1General instructions

  1. Form your group of 2 to 3 students (maximum 4) and tell the teacher your topic by Thursday, October 22. Each group needs a different topic.
  2. Choose a real application in engineering, science or society that uses Uniformly Accelerated Motion and Newton's Laws.
  3. Research how the physics works in that application. Use at least 3 reliable sources.
  4. Create a Canva presentation of 8 to 12 slides with short text, images and a diagram made by your group.
  5. Include one worked calculation with real data: the data, the equation, the substitution and the result with SI units.
  6. Upload the Canva link to the Google Classroom assignment before class on October 28. Every member uploads it, and the link must be set so the teacher can view it.
  7. Present in class on October 28, in English. Every member speaks.

2Example of what we are looking for

Sample project

Why speed limits in school zones save lives

A group investigates how the speed of a car changes the distance it needs to stop. They connect braking to UAM and the braking force to Newton's Second Law. Their slides could look like this:

Slide 1Title, group members, section and date
Slide 2The question: how far does a car travel before it stops?
Slide 3The physics: deceleration, inertia and net force
Slide 4How brakes and tires stop a car
Slide 5Free-body diagram of the braking car
Slide 6Calculation: 40 km/h vs 60 km/h
Slide 7Conclusion and a road-safety recommendation
Slide 8Sources
Sample calculation (Slide 6). A 1200 kg car brakes with a deceleration of 6.0 m/s². Compare 40 km/h (11.1 m/s) and 60 km/h (16.7 m/s).
d = (vf² − vi²) / (2a) = (0 − 11.1²) / (2 · (−6.0)) ≈ 10.3 m
d = (0 − 16.7²) / (2 · (−6.0)) ≈ 23.2 m
Fnet = m · a = 1200 kg · 6.0 m/s² = 7200 N

Conclusion: 50% more speed needs more than twice the braking distance. That is why school zones have low speed limits.

This is only an example. Your group must choose a different topic and use its own data.

6What your presentation must include

  1. Title slide: project title, group members, section and date.
  2. The question: what real situation you investigated and why it matters.
  3. The physics: the concepts and laws involved, explained in your own words.
  4. How it works: the real application in engineering, science or society.
  5. A diagram: a free-body diagram or a motion diagram made by your group.
  6. One worked calculation: real data, the equation, the substitution and the result with SI units.
  7. Conclusion: what you learned and one recommendation for people or society.
  8. Sources: at least 3 reliable sources (books, science websites, universities or agencies like NASA).

Use short text and large letters. Write the content in your own words and cite every image, video or tool you use.
